CrackNet — Perception Pipeline

How raw photons become a crack mask, on a Jetson Orin Nano with a Logitech C920.

Scope

This document covers perception, not learning. The segmentation network is treated as a fixed black box with a defined input and output contract. What is documented here is everything on either side of it: how light becomes a correctly-exposed, correctly-framed, correctly-scaled tensor, and how the network's raw probability field becomes a stable decision.

Training, architecture, loss functions and dataset construction are deliberately out of scope. They live in the Colab notebooks and in setup.md.txt §0.

The framing that matters: the network is the smallest source of error in this system. TensorRT agrees with ONNX Runtime to within 0.0552 % of mask pixels. Every large failure observed in deployment came from perception — a saturated sensor, an unstable control loop, a geometrically wrong crop, or a post-processing step that could not distinguish a filament from a blob.

1 · Acquisition

Why the pixel format is a perception decision

The C920 offers two formats over USB 2.0, whose practical ceiling is around 480 Mbit/s shared with protocol overhead. Uncompressed YUYV at 1280×720 is 16 bits per pixel:

1280 × 720 × 16 bits × 30 fps ≈ 442 Mbit/s

That does not fit, so the camera silently negotiates down to roughly 10 fps. MJPG compresses in-camera and sustains 30 fps. The cost is JPEG quantisation — an 8×8 block DCT that discards high-frequency detail, which is precisely where a hairline crack lives. This is a real trade: we accept mild high-frequency loss to avoid a 3× frame-rate loss, because motion blur from a slow frame rate destroys more crack evidence than JPEG does.

Ordering trap

The FOURCC must be set before width and height. Set afterwards, the driver has already committed to YUYV and silently caps at ~10 fps, with no error anywhere. A frame rate that is quietly 3× too low is the hardest kind of bug to notice, because everything still works.

# live.py
def open_c920(dev=0, w=1280, h=720, fps=30):
    cap = cv2.VideoCapture(dev, cv2.CAP_V4L2)
    cap.set(cv2.CAP_PROP_FOURCC, cv2.VideoWriter_fourcc(*"MJPG"))  # must come first
    cap.set(cv2.CAP_PROP_FRAME_WIDTH,  w)
    cap.set(cv2.CAP_PROP_FRAME_HEIGHT, h)
    cap.set(cv2.CAP_PROP_FPS, fps)
    cap.set(cv2.CAP_PROP_BUFFERSIZE, 1)   # prefer the newest frame
    if not cap.isOpened():
        raise RuntimeError(f"cannot open /dev/video{dev}")
    for _ in range(5):        # get the stream actually running first
        cap.read()
    lock_camera_controls(dev)
    return cap

Latency versus freshness

CAP_PROP_BUFFERSIZE = 1 asks the driver to keep the queue shallow. In a perception loop, a stale frame is worse than a dropped one: acting on 100 ms-old evidence means the overlay describes where the camera was. Note the caveat — this reduces queueing but does not suppress the transient frames discussed in stage 2.

The five-frame prelude

The loop reads and discards five frames before applying controls. The C920's firmware drops out of manual mode during stream initialisation and ignores exposure set before streaming begins — independently reported on the linux-media list, and observed here as exposure_time_absolute silently reverting 156 → 312. Controls applied to a camera that is not yet streaming do not stick.

2 · Photometric control

The core argument: clipping is irreversible

A crack is a low-contrast dark line on a brighter surface. Detection depends entirely on the difference between crack pixels and surround. When the surround saturates, every pixel there reads 255 regardless of the true radiance — the mapping from scene to sensor stops being injective, and the contrast carrying the crack is destroyed at the sensor.

Why no filter can fix it

CLAHE, histogram equalisation and gamma all operate on recorded values. Once a region is uniformly 255, there is no recorded variation to stretch. Contrast enhancement of clipped data amplifies quantisation and noise into crack-like filaments — it makes false positives, not detections. The only place this is fixable is exposure.

Two actuators, not one

ControlUnit / rangeEffectCost
exposure_time_absolute100 µs units, 3–2047 Integration time. Linear in radiance. Motion blur; caps frame rate (2047 = 205 ms ⇒ ~5 fps)
gain0–255Analog amplification after capture Amplifies sensor noise — thin bright streaks, i.e. false cracks

Both raise brightness; they are not interchangeable. Exposure is nearly free until motion blur sets in, gain is never free. Hence a strict ladder: exposure is always preferred, gain is the reserve. On first contact this camera was found at gain = 255 — its maximum, default 0 — left there by the firmware's own auto-exposure before manual mode was set.

Choice of control metric

The loop needs one scalar per frame. The choice determines whether it converges.

MetricBehaviourVerdict
Mean lumaDragged by large bright or dark regions; a white wall reads high by definitionWeak on uniform surfaces
99th percentile → 235Aims highlights just under clipping. But on a bimodal scene (dark room + bright window) p99 is either ~216 or saturated and never lands on targetFailed — limit cycle, exposure swung 311 ↔ 532
Median → 120Robust to small bright regions; represents the inspected surface, which fills most of the frameUsed. Always reachable, so the loop converges
Principle

A control target must be reachable. An unreachable setpoint makes an integrating controller wind up indefinitely — and the instinctive fix, clamping the output, is what produced the worst bug in this project.

The control law

Luma is approximately linear in exposure, so target / median is a Newton step. It is damped (exponent 0.6) and the per-step ratio is clamped so one bad frame cannot cause a large jump.

# camera_ctl.py — core of update()
med     = self.med_ema                       # smoothed, see below
clipped = float((gray >= 254).mean())

# if much of the frame is blown out, aim lower until it recovers
target = self.target * (0.8 if clipped > self.clip_guard else 1.0)
err    = med - target

if abs(err) <= self.tol:                     # deadband: do not hunt
    # converged - but trade gain down for exposure if possible
    if self.gain > GAIN_MIN and self.exp < self.exp_cap:
        self._apply_gain(self.gain - min(8, self.gain))
        self._apply_exposure(self.exp * 1.2)
    return

ratio = float(np.clip(target / max(med, 1.0), 0.4, 2.5)) ** self.damp

if err < 0:                                  # too dark - brighten
    if   self.exp  < self.exp_cap:  self._apply_exposure(self.exp * ratio)
    elif self.gain < self.gain_cap: self._apply_gain(self.gain + gstep)
else:                                       # too bright - darken
    if   self.gain > GAIN_MIN:      self._apply_gain(self.gain - gstep)
    else:                           self._apply_exposure(self.exp * ratio)

Read the ladder carefully — the ordering is the safety property. Brightening touches gain only at maximum exposure; darkening touches exposure only at zero gain. The pathological state (minimum exposure with maximum gain) is therefore unreachable, not merely unlikely.

Deadband, and why convergence is not the end

Without a deadband the loop oscillates around the setpoint forever, and every actuation costs frames. With one, the loop stops when close enough. But stopping is not sufficient: the ladder can converge on correct brightness at a noisy operating point — measured once at exposure=71, gain=111. The deadband branch therefore performs a slow quality migration, trading gain down and exposure up at constant brightness until gain reaches zero. Same luma, less noise.

Actuation lag and transient frames

The single hardest observation in this project:

exposure=500  gain=0   median=  4     # nearly black
exposure=500  gain=0   median=241     # same settings, nearly white

Identical actuator state, opposite measurements. The C920 emits black or blown frames for several frames after any control change, and a change takes a few frames to take effect at all. A controller that measures during this window is reading noise and reacting to it — which is how a stable loop becomes a thrashing one.

Three defences, all required:

  1. Flush — cap.grab() discards in-flight frames after every change.
  2. Blind period — no measurement for ~0.5 s after a change.
  3. EMA smoothing — med = 0.5·med + 0.5·new, so a single surviving transient cannot move the estimate far.

Testing a control loop you cannot hold still

With a person moving in frame, the median swings 19 ↔ 178 at fixed settings. Any live pass/fail is measuring the room, not the controller. The control law is therefore tested against a simulated sensor that reproduces the response curve, the actuation lag and the transient frames:

def read(self):
    self._tick()
    if self.since_change <= self.garbage:      # transient after a change
        v = 0 if self.rng.random() < 0.5 else 255
        return True, np.full((64, 64, 3), v, np.uint8)
    luma = 255.0 * self.reflectance * (self.exp / 250.0) * (1 + self.gain / 48.0)
    return True, np.full((64, 64, 3), int(np.clip(luma, 0, 255)), np.uint8)

Seven scenarios including both saturation extremes and the exact stuck state seen in the field. All pass. The on-camera test is then a smoke test, not the specification.

3 · Geometric normalisation

Crop, do not stretch

The camera delivers 16:9; the model consumes 1:1. Resizing 1280×720 directly to 512×512 scales x by 0.40 and y by 0.71 — an anisotropic transform. A crack at 45° changes apparent angle, and crack width becomes direction-dependent: the same physical fissure measures differently horizontally and vertically.

The model never saw that during training, and width is the feature stage 6 filters on. Anisotropic scaling would corrupt both the network's input statistics and our own geometric reasoning. Centre-cropping preserves the aspect ratio exactly; the cost is losing the left and right thirds of the field of view.

def center_square(frame):
    """Crop the central HxH square so the model sees undistorted geometry."""
    h, w = frame.shape[:2]
    s = min(h, w)
    y0, x0 = (h - s) // 2, (w - s) // 2
    return frame[y0:y0 + s, x0:x0 + s], (x0, y0, s)

Resampling: why INTER_AREA

720 → 512 is a 0.711 downscale. Downsampling without a low-pass filter aliases: high-frequency detail folds back as false low-frequency structure — and a thin crack is high-frequency detail. INTER_LINEAR samples four neighbours and misses most source pixels. INTER_AREA averages over the full source footprint of each destination pixel, which is the correct anti-aliasing operation for minification.

The practical difference: with linear interpolation a hairline crack flickers in and out between frames as sub-pixel alignment shifts. With area averaging it dims but stays present.

The resolution budget

This constrains the whole system and is worth stating numerically. A crack 2 px wide in the 720×720 crop becomes:

2 px × (512 / 720) = 1.42 px

Near the sampling limit, before JPEG quantisation. This is why standoff distance matters more than any parameter: CRACK500 was photographed close enough that cracks span many pixels. Doubling the distance halves crack width in pixels and can push it below detectability regardless of how well everything else is tuned.

If full width is needed

Split the frame into two overlapping 720×720 tiles and stitch the masks. Costs 2× inference — affordable here, since at 18.4 ms the loop is camera-bound at ~22 fps, not compute-bound.

4 · Colour and numeric normalisation

Four transforms, in a fixed order, forming the input contract. Any deviation produces confident nonsense rather than an error — the network accepts whatever shape it is given.

# trt_infer.py
MEAN = np.array([0.485, 0.456, 0.406], np.float32)
STD  = np.array([0.229, 0.224, 0.225], np.float32)

@staticmethod
def preprocess(rgb):
    """rgb: HxWx3 uint8 already resized to SIZE x SIZE -> 1x3xHxW float32."""
    x = (rgb.astype(np.float32) / 255.0 - MEAN) / STD
    return np.ascontiguousarray(x.transpose(2, 0, 1)[None])
StepOperationFailure if omitted
Channel ordercv2.COLOR_BGR2RGB R and B swap. The encoder's colour statistics invert; output is plausible-looking garbage. Classic symptom: the whole frame lights up.
Scale/ 255.0Inputs 255× too large; saturates activations.
Standardise(x − μ) / σ, ImageNet statistics The encoder is timm-mobilenetv3_large_100, pretrained on ImageNet. Its learned filters expect that distribution.
LayoutHWC → CHW, add batch axisShape mismatch, or a silently misread tensor.

np.ascontiguousarray is not cosmetic. transpose returns a view with permuted strides; the memory order is unchanged. The subsequent memcpy_htod copies raw bytes to the GPU with no knowledge of strides, so a non-contiguous array would transfer correctly-sized, incorrectly-ordered data — and TensorRT would happily infer on it.

An equivalence worth knowing

Thresholding the sigmoid at 0.55 is identical to thresholding the raw logit:

logit(0.55) = ln(0.55 / 0.45) = 0.2007

Measured, the numpy sigmoid over 512×512 costs 2.46 ms; the logit comparison costs 0.07 ms. Since only the mask is used downstream — never the probability values themselves — this is a free 2.4 ms. Currently unapplied: the loop is camera-bound, so it would not raise frame rate.

One property that shapes all downstream perception

The model cannot abstain

It is a single-channel sigmoid with no "none of the above" output, trained on a dataset where every image contains a crack. Its effective prior is there is a crack here, find it. Shown a person it does not report absence — it partitions the frame into more- and less-crack-like regions and commits.

Measured: 19 % coverage on an indoor scene, against the 6.3 % that is crack in an average CRACK500 mask. The false positive was three times denser than a real crack. No threshold fixes a missing capability.

Stage 6 exists because of this property. It is a mitigation, not a repair.

Quantisation is not the problem

FP16 is 2.1× faster than FP32 (8.77 ms vs 18.44 ms GPU compute). The parity check bounds the cost: 0.0552 % of mask pixels disagree with ONNX Runtime, against a 0.1 % acceptance bar, with 38 % of pixels above threshold — so the test exercised the model rather than passing vacuously.

6 · Mask geometry

Area is not enough

The original filter dropped components below min_area. That removes specks but cannot distinguish one large crack from one large blob — they have the same area. The distinguishing property is not size but shape: cracks are thin, elongated, branching filaments; out-of-domain false positives are fat compact regions.

Two measures

Mean half-width = area / perimeter

For a stroke of width w and length L: area ≈ wL, perimeter ≈ 2L. So area/perimeter ≈ w/2 — a direct read of thickness in pixels, independent of length, orientation and curvature.

Solidity = area / convex-hull area

A meandering filament's convex hull is far larger than the filament, giving low solidity. A blob fills most of its hull, giving solidity near 1. Applied only to large components, because a short stubby but genuine crack can legitimately be solid.

Measured separation

PopulationMean half-widthSolidityOutcome
Synthetic thin crack (control)1.94 px 0.041kept
Real false blobs (51 components)median 11.2, max 43 median 0.715, max 1.09rejected

Effect: 96.2 % of false-positive coverage removed, 38 % → 1.44 %, with the synthetic filament fully intact.

# live.py — clean_mask()
n, lab, stats, _ = cv2.connectedComponentsWithStats(mask, connectivity=8)
for i in range(1, n):
    area = int(stats[i, cv2.CC_STAT_AREA])
    if area < min_area:                      continue
    if area > max_area_frac * total:         continue   # too big to be a crack

    sub  = (lab[y:y+h, x:x+w] == i).astype(np.uint8)   # ROI, not whole frame
    cnts, _ = cv2.findContours(sub, cv2.RETR_EXTERNAL, cv2.CHAIN_APPROX_NONE)
    perim = sum(cv2.arcLength(c, True) for c in cnts)

    if area / max(perim, 1.0) > max_halfwidth:  continue  # too thick
    if area > 0.02 * total:
        hull = cv2.convexHull(max(cnts, key=cv2.contourArea))
        if area / cv2.contourArea(hull) > max_solidity: continue  # compact
    keep[lab == i] = 255
Trap: contourArea on thin structures

Area comes from CC_STAT_AREA — the connected-component pixel count — and never from cv2.contourArea. For a 1 px wide line the closed contour encloses almost no region, so contourArea returns ≈ 0. Using it would reject exactly the structures we are trying to detect. arcLength on the same contour is fine; perimeter is well-defined for a degenerate polygon.

Threshold selection, and a near-miss

The false blobs cluster at half-width 11, so 8 px looks like an obvious threshold. It is wrong. CRACK500 masks average 6.3 % of a 512² frame; if that is one filament, the implied width is around 25 px — half-width ≈ 12. Setting 8 would have silently discarded wide cracks, with no error and no symptom except lower recall on exactly the defects that matter most.

Kept at 12, favouring recall. Documented as a floor. There are no in-domain crack images on the device to validate a tighter value against, and a parameter that cannot be validated should not be tightened.

CHAIN_APPROX_NONE rather than SIMPLE: the latter collapses straight runs to endpoints, which changes the computed perimeter and therefore the half-width. The filter measures geometry, so it needs every boundary point.

7 · Temporal integration

Per-frame decisions flicker. Sensor noise, autofocus micro-adjustments and hand movement all push borderline pixels across the threshold between consecutive frames. A detector that alternates CRACK/clear at 15 Hz is unusable regardless of per-frame accuracy.

frac     = float((mask > 0).mean())          # this frame's coverage
smooth   = 0.7 * smooth + 0.3 * frac         # exponential moving average
is_crack = smooth > args.alert_frac

An EMA with α = 0.3 has an effective memory of roughly 1/α ≈ 3 frames — at 15 fps, about 200 ms. Long enough to suppress single-frame noise, short enough that the display tracks camera motion. It also costs one float of state, which matters in a loop already spending 18.4 ms per frame.

Setting the alert fraction

The guide's 0.2 % sits uncomfortably close to the noise floor. 1 % is an order of magnitude above the speck level and still well below a real crack, leaving headroom for small ones. This is the single most deployment-dependent parameter in the system.

8 · Rendering and transport

Mask upsampling: nearest, not linear

big = cv2.resize(mask, (crop.shape[1], crop.shape[0]),
                 interpolation=cv2.INTER_NEAREST)
vis = crop.copy()
vis[big > 0] = (0.4 * vis[big > 0] + 0.6 * np.array([0, 0, 255])).astype(np.uint8)
contours, _ = cv2.findContours(big, cv2.RETR_EXTERNAL, cv2.CHAIN_APPROX_SIMPLE)
cv2.drawContours(vis, contours, -1, (0, 255, 255), 1)

The mask is binary. Interpolating it linearly would invent intermediate values along every edge, so the displayed boundary would no longer be the boundary the decision was made on. INTER_NEAREST keeps the overlay an honest rendering of the actual mask.

The 0.4/0.6 alpha blend keeps surface texture visible through the highlight, so an operator can judge whether the detection sits on a real feature. A solid fill would hide the evidence. The yellow contour is drawn separately because a blend alone makes it hard to see where a detection ends.

Note the BGR ordering — [0,0,255] is red and (0,255,255) is yellow, because OpenCV draws in BGR.

Decoupling observation from measurement

A naïve server would run inference inside the HTTP handler: two viewers would double the load and halve the frame rate, so the act of watching would change what is measured. Instead one producer thread owns the camera and publishes the latest encoded JPEG; handlers only read it.

with self.lock:
    self.jpeg   = buf.tobytes()
    self.seq   += 1
    self.status = {...}
    self.lock.notify_all()          # wake every waiting viewer

def latest(self, last_seq, timeout=5.0):
    """Block until a frame newer than last_seq exists."""
    with self.lock:
        if self.seq == last_seq:
            self.lock.wait(timeout)
        return self.jpeg, self.seq

The sequence number makes each viewer wait for a frame it has not sent, rather than polling or re-sending the same one. N viewers cost N socket writes and zero extra inference.

CUDA contexts are thread-affine

pycuda.autoinit creates the context on the importing thread. A CUDA context belongs to one thread at a time, so the producer thread must push() it before touching TensorRT — otherwise every call fails with invalid device context. This is a genuine constraint of the runtime, not a quirk of this design.

MJPEG over HTTP

multipart/x-mixed-replace is a single HTTP response that never ends, each part a complete JPEG replacing the last. Every browser renders it in a plain <img>, with no JavaScript, no WebRTC and no plugin. The trade is bandwidth — each frame is independent, with no inter-frame compression. At ~50 KB and 15 fps that is roughly 6 Mbit/s: fine on a LAN, wrong for the open internet.

It also suits the task. Inter-frame codecs reconstruct predicted frames from reference frames, so a dropped packet smears artefacts across subsequent frames — artefacts an operator could mistake for a detection. Independent frames degrade by dropping whole frames, which is visually obvious and unambiguous.

The recurring lesson

Four of the eight are cases where a component reported success while being wrong: a frame rate quietly 3× low, an exposure control that accepted a value and then reverted, a controller happily converged in a pathological state, and a model confidently segmenting a person. Perception systems fail silently far more often than they fail loudly, which is why nearly every number in this document is measured rather than assumed.

Anticipated questions

The harder questions this design invites, with honest answers.

Why not just use the camera's own auto-exposure?

It optimises for a pleasing picture, not for crack contrast — it will happily blow out a white wall to lift the shadows. It also hunts continuously, so exposure changes mid-inspection and coverage figures are not comparable between frames. Manual mode with an explicit target makes the photometric state known and reproducible. The cost is having to write and test a control loop, which is where most of this project's effort went.

Isn't the shape filter just hiding the model's mistakes?

Yes, and that is stated explicitly. It removes 96 % of false-positive coverage without making the model any better — the network still produces those blobs confidently. Anything with elongated dark structure (wood grain, grout lines, cables, wrinkled fabric) will pass a thinness test. The real fix is a surface-gate classifier or fine-tuning with hard negatives; the filter buys a usable system in the meantime.

Why a median and not a mean for exposure control?

Robustness to outlier regions. A window occupying 5 % of the frame at 255 moves the mean substantially and the median almost not at all. Since the inspected surface fills most of the frame, the median tracks the thing we care about exposing correctly. The mean would let a bright background dictate the exposure of the surface.

The blob thresholds came from 51 components on indoor scenes. Is that enough?

Enough to establish separation — the two populations differ by roughly 6× in half-width and 17× in solidity, which is not a marginal result. Not enough to tighten the thresholds, which is why 12 px is documented as a floor rather than a tuned value. Proper calibration needs in-domain pavement images with ground-truth masks; the analysis script exists so it can be re-run when they are available.

Why centre-crop and discard two-thirds of the frame?

Because anisotropic scaling corrupts crack width, which is the feature the shape filter measures, and the model never saw distorted geometry in training. Full coverage is available by tiling — two overlapping 720×720 tiles at 2× inference cost, affordable since the loop is camera-bound at ~22 fps rather than compute-bound at 18.4 ms.

Why is FP16 acceptable when it changes the output at all?

Because the change was measured rather than assumed: 0.0552 % of mask pixels differ from the ONNX Runtime reference, against a 0.1 % bar, on a sample where 38 % of pixels were above threshold. For a 2.1× speedup that is a good trade. The FP32 engine is retained specifically so the comparison can be repeated.

What limits frame rate, and why not optimise it?

The camera. Raw MJPG capture tops out at ~22 fps because JPEG decode runs on CPU; with inference the loop lands at 15–19 fps. Inference is 18.4 ms, of which 2.46 ms is the numpy sigmoid that could be removed entirely by thresholding in logit space. That optimisation is deliberately not applied — it would not raise frame rate, because the bottleneck is elsewhere. Hardware JPEG decode (NVJPG) is the lever that would actually help.

Why test the exposure loop in simulation rather than on the real camera?

Because the real scene is not controllable. With someone moving in frame the median swings 19 ↔ 178 at fixed settings, so a live pass/fail measures the room. The simulation fixes the sensor response and reproduces the two behaviours that actually broke earlier versions — actuation lag and transient frames — giving a deterministic, repeatable result. The on-camera test is retained as a smoke test.

What would you do next, given more time?

In order: (1) validate on real pavement, since every number here except the parity check was measured on out-of-domain indoor scenes; (2) add the surface gate, which is the only principled fix for non-pavement false positives; (3) hardware JPEG decode to lift the capture ceiling; (4) tile the full frame width. Re-exporting at 448 to match training resolution is listed in the guide but is the weakest of these — it saves 23 % of compute the system is not short of.
